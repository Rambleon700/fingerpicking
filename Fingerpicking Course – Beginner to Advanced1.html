Basics: set up before you play

Ten minutes here saves months later.

Position

  • Seated: upright on a firm chair, guitar body resting on your right thigh (right-handed), neck angled slightly up. Classical players raise the left foot on a footstool and rest the guitar on the left thigh. Either is fine; choose the one that keeps your shoulders level.
  • Right hand: forearm rests on the guitar’s upper bout. Wrist gently arched, not collapsed. Fingers curl toward the palm and pluck across and slightly up, not straight down into the soundboard.
  • Thumb: sits slightly in front of (towards the headstock side of) the fingers, so that thumb and fingers never collide.
  • Nails: a little free edge on i, m and a gives a clear tone. Filed smooth and rounded to follow the fingertip. Fleshy fingertips sound softer and work well too.
Avoid planting the little finger on the top as an anchor. A light touch is fine at first, but a heavy anchor locks the hand and limits reach.

Finger names and strings

Classical letters are used everywhere in this course. The strings are numbered 1 (thinnest, high e) to 6 (thickest, low E).

How to read the lessons

  • Each column is one eighth note. The count above is 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &, so eight columns make one bar of 4/4. Say it out loud while you play.
  • The top line of the tab is string 1, the bottom is string 6. A number is the fret; 0 is an open string. Amber dots are thumb notes; blue dots are fingers. The letter underneath is the picking finger.
  • The chord box on the left shows where the left hand goes. Hold the whole shape down for the entire bar, even if some notes are not played yet.
  • Press Play to hear and watch the pattern. Change the tempo in the bar at the top. Tick “mastered” when you can play it cleanly for 2 minutes at 60 BPM.

Free stroke and rest stroke

In a free stroke the finger plucks and swings clear above the next string. It is the default in fingerstyle, giving a light, ringing tone. In a rest stroke the finger plucks and comes to rest on the next string, giving a fuller, louder note, which is useful for a melody you want to stand out. Start every lesson with free stroke.

Artificial harmonics (technique, no tab)

A harmonic is a bell-like note made by touching a string lightly at a node instead of pressing it down. Natural harmonics sit over the 12th, 7th and 5th frets. Artificial harmonics let you play them on any note.

  1. Fret a note normally with the left hand, say the 3rd fret of string 5 (a C).
  2. Touch the same string with the right-hand index finger lightly 12 frets higher, which is the 15th fret here. Touch only; do not press.
  3. Pluck with a (or p) just behind your touching finger, then lift the index finger straight away.
If you hear a dull click, the touch is too heavy. If it sounds like a normal note, you are on the wrong spot. Slide the index finger a millimetre either way until it rings.

Practice that works

30–45 minute routine

  1. 5 min: open-string p-i-m-a and slow chord changes to warm up.
  2. 10 min: the one pattern you are working on, starting at 50 BPM.
  3. 10 min: chord changes in a progression, looped.
  4. 5–10 min: add 4 BPM only if the last pass was clean.
  5. Last: play something you love, without the click.

Fixing common problems

  • Thumb rushes or drags: play the thumb alone with the click for two minutes, then add one finger.
  • Fingers hit the wrong string: prepare the fingers on their strings before plucking (plant), and lift off only after the note.
  • Notes are uneven in volume: aim to make the thumb slightly louder than the fingers. Count aloud so the off-beats stay lighter.
  • Chord changes break the rhythm: change the shape on the last “&” and keep the thumb going. The pause is the bass note, not the hand.
  • Tension in the hand: stop, shake out, drop the tempo by 15 BPM.

Using the patterns in songs

One pattern plus one progression is a whole song. Try C–G–Am–Fmaj7 (lesson 7) under any melody you like to sing. The classic fingerstyle repertoire (for example Dust in the Wind-type arpeggios, Blackbird’s paired strings, ballad intros built on open Em) is made from these same moves. Learn those pieces from a licensed score; chord progressions and techniques here are free to use, but the notes of published songs are not reproduced.
